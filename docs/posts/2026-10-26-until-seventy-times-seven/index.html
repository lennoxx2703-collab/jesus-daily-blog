Until Seventy Times Seven

October 26, 2026 · Midday · 4 min read

Scripture Matthew 18:21–27

  • monday
  • forgiveness
  • mercy
  • relationships
  • midday

It’s barely lunchtime on a Monday, and most of us are already carrying something. A colleague who took credit again. A family message that landed wrong. The person who always does that one thing, and did it again this morning. None of it is huge. But by noon it can sit in the chest like a stone.

Peter knew that feeling. One day he brought it straight to Jesus.

“Then came Peter to him, and said, Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times? Jesus saith unto him, I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven.” (Matthew 18:21–22, KJV)

I think Peter thought he was being generous. Seven times is a lot. Most of us stop at two. He was asking for a limit, a number he could reach, after which he’d be free to stop.

Jesus gives him a number so big it stops being a number. Not “keep count up to 490,” but “stop counting.” Forgiveness in His kingdom isn’t a ledger you eventually close. It’s a way of living.

A debt no one could pay

Then Jesus tells a story, because He knows commands alone don’t change hearts.

“Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened unto a certain king, which would take account of his servants. And when he had begun to reckon, one was brought unto him, which owed him ten thousand talents. But forasmuch as he had not to pay, his lord commanded him to be sold, and his wife, and children, and all that he had, and payment to be made.” (Matthew 18:23–25, KJV)

Ten thousand talents was an absurd sum, more than a working man could earn in many lifetimes. Jesus is being deliberately over the top. This servant isn’t a little behind. He’s buried.

“The servant therefore fell down, and worshipped him, saying, Lord, have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. Then the lord of that servant was moved with compassion, and loosed him, and forgave him the debt.” (Matthew 18:26–27, KJV)

Notice what the servant asks for: patience. More time. He still thinks he can work it off. What he gets is far more than he asked: the lord was moved with compassion, and loosed him, and forgave him the debt. Not a payment plan. Freedom. The whole thing, gone.

That’s the gospel in one sentence. We came to God hoping for a little more time to get our act together, and He cancelled the debt.

Why it matters at noon

You probably know how the story continues. The forgiven servant walks out, finds a man who owes him a small amount, and grabs him by the throat. Jesus tells it so we’ll see ourselves in it: freshly forgiven, and still clutching someone else’s little debt.

The point isn’t “forgive, or else.” The point is that forgiveness flows out of having been forgiven. When I remember the size of what Jesus has let go for me, the colleague’s comment shrinks back to its real size. It still hurt. It may still need an honest conversation. But I don’t have to carry it into the afternoon like a bill I’m waiting to collect.

Forgiving isn’t pretending nothing happened. It isn’t letting someone keep harming you, and it doesn’t always mean trust comes back right away. It means handing the debt to God and refusing to make it the center of your day.

For this afternoon

  • Name the stone. What did you pick up this morning? Say it plainly to Jesus. He’s not shocked.
  • Remember your ten thousand talents. Take a moment to think of what He has forgiven you. Not to feel crushed, but to feel loosed.
  • Let go of one small debt. Just one, just for today. If it comes back at 3 p.m., let it go again. That’s what seventy times seven looks like in practice: again, and again, and again.

“And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.” (Ephesians 4:32, KJV)

Lord Jesus, You forgave me a debt I could never pay. I’m holding onto small ones this morning, and they’re heavy. Help me hand them to You. Make me tenderhearted this afternoon, and when the hurt comes back, help me forgive again. Amen.

A prayer

Jesus, I bring You the weight of this hour. Meet me in Matthew 18:21–27, and let Your word rest in my heart. Give rest to my soul and strength for what remains.
