Jesus Wept

October 10, 2026 · Evening · 4 min read

Scripture John 11:35

  • grief
  • comfort
  • presence
  • hope

Saturday evenings have a way of going quiet. The errands are done, the noise of the week has faded, and if you’re carrying a loss, this is often the hour it finds you. A chair nobody sits in. A phone number you still haven’t deleted. A “what if” that won’t let go.

Tonight I want to stand with you at a grave in Bethany, because Jesus stood there too.

Four days too late

Lazarus was sick, and his sisters, Mary and Martha, sent word to Jesus. They knew He loved their brother. They knew what He could do. And yet Jesus didn’t arrive in time. By the time He reached the village, Lazarus had been in the tomb for four days.

Both sisters said the same thing to Him, word for word. Here is Mary:

“Lord, if you would have been here, my brother wouldn’t have died.” (John 11:32)

I love that the Bible keeps that sentence. It’s not polite. It’s not a tidy prayer. It’s grief speaking honestly to God: You could have stopped this. Where were You? If you’ve ever prayed something like that, you’re in good company. Mary said it at His feet, and He didn’t send her away.

What Jesus did first

Here’s what stops me every time. Jesus already knew how this story would end. A few verses earlier He had told Martha, “I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies” (John 11:25). In a few minutes He would call a dead man out of a grave.

He could have skipped straight to the miracle. He could have said, “Dry your eyes, watch this.” Instead, John tells us:

“When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews weeping who came with her, he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled… Jesus wept.” (John 11:33, 35)

Two words. The shortest verse in most English Bibles, and maybe one of the deepest.

Jesus wept. Not because He was confused or hopeless, but because He loved them, and death is wrong, and their pain was real to Him. The people watching understood it immediately: “See how much affection he had for him!” (John 11:36).

That tells us something about who God is. He isn’t a distant fixer who looks at our grief as a problem to be solved quickly. He comes close enough to be troubled by it. He lets our tears become His tears before He ever says a word about tomorrow.

You don’t have to hurry past the hurt

Sometimes we think faith means getting over sadness fast. We say the right verses and push the feelings down, as if tears were a sign we don’t trust Him enough.

But Jesus, who trusted the Father perfectly, cried at a funeral.

So if tonight you’re missing someone, or mourning a dream, a marriage, a season of health, or a version of your life that didn’t happen, you’re allowed to feel it. You’re allowed to say, “Lord, if You had been here…” He can hold that sentence. He has heard it before, and He answered it with presence.

And then, the loud voice

The story doesn’t end at the tears. Jesus walked to the tomb, asked them to roll the stone away, prayed, and then “cried with a loud voice, ‘Lazarus, come out!’” (John 11:43). And the man who had been dead walked out, still wrapped in grave clothes.

Both things are true about Jesus at once: He weeps with us, and He is the resurrection and the life. The tears are not the opposite of hope. They’re what hope looks like when it loves you enough to stand beside you in the dark first.

A small step for tonight

Before you sleep, tell Jesus plainly what you’re grieving. Use your own words, even blunt ones. Then sit for a moment with this picture: He is not waiting impatiently for you to be done. He’s standing there with you, and He’s not dry-eyed.

Lord Jesus, You wept at Lazarus’s grave, so I know You see what I’ve lost. Stay close to me tonight. Hold what I can’t fix. And let me rest in the hope that You are the resurrection and the life. Amen.

A prayer

Stay with us, Lord, for it is toward evening. Meet me in John 11:35, and let Your word rest in my heart. Remain with us through the night, and keep our hearts burning.
