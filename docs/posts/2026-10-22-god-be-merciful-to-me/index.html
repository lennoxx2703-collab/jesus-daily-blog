God Be Merciful to Me

October 22, 2026 · Midday · 3 min read

Scripture Luke 18:10–14

  • thursday
  • prayer
  • humility
  • mercy
  • midday

Halfway through the day, most of us are keeping some kind of score. What got done, what didn’t. Who was easy to work with and who wasn’t. Whether we’re doing better or worse than the person at the next desk. It happens so quietly we barely notice it.

Jesus told a story for people who keep score like that.

“Two men went up into the temple to pray; the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican. The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.” (Luke 18:10–12, KJV)

On paper, the Pharisee is the good one. He’s disciplined. He’s generous. He shows up. If you were picking someone to run the church budget, you’d probably pick him.

But listen to how Luke puts it: he “prayed thus with himself.” His prayer starts with God’s name and then turns into a mirror. Every line is about him, and most of it is about how he compares. He even uses the other man in the room as a measuring stick: “or even as this publican.”

Then there’s the tax collector.

“And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner.” (Luke 18:13, KJV)

He stands at the back. He can’t look up. He doesn’t list anything, because he knows his list wouldn’t help him. Everyone in that temple knew what tax collectors were: men who worked for Rome and skimmed off their own people. He doesn’t argue with that. He just asks for mercy. Seven words.

And Jesus says something that must have shocked the people listening:

“I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.” (Luke 18:14, KJV)

The man with nothing to offer went home right with God. The man with everything to offer went home the same as he came.

Mercy isn’t earned at the front of the room

I don’t think Jesus is telling us that fasting and giving are bad. They’re good things. The problem is what the Pharisee did with them. He turned his good habits into a ladder, and once you’re on a ladder, all you can see is who’s below you.

The tax collector had no ladder. So he did the only thing left: he let God come down to him. And that’s exactly what God loves to do. “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise” (Psalm 51:17, KJV).

That’s good news on a Thursday afternoon when you’re tired and the morning didn’t go the way you hoped. You don’t have to come to Jesus with a strong report. You don’t have to be ahead of anyone. You can come from the back of the room with your eyes down and still be heard.

A midday pause

If you have a minute right now, try this.

Notice where you’ve been comparing today. Maybe you’ve been quietly thinking you’re better than someone. Maybe you’ve been quietly thinking you’re worse than everyone. Both are the same scoreboard, and Jesus invites you to put it down.

Then pray the tax collector’s prayer, slowly, as your own: God, be merciful to me, a sinner. Not as a way of beating yourself up, but as a way of telling the truth and resting in His answer. He doesn’t despise that prayer. He has never once turned it away.

And if someone comes to mind who you’ve been measuring yourself against, pray for them too. It’s hard to look down on a person you’re bringing to God.

The afternoon is still ahead. You can walk into it the way that man walked home: not proud, not crushed, just forgiven.

A prayer

Jesus, I bring You the weight of this hour. Meet me in Luke 18:10–14, and let Your word rest in my heart. Give rest to my soul and strength for what remains.
