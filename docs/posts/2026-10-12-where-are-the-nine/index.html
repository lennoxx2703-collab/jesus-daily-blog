Where Are the Nine?

October 12, 2026 · Midday · 4 min read

Scripture Luke 17:17

  • gratitude
  • healing
  • thanksgiving
  • faith

By midday the day has already handed us gifts we barely noticed. A safe commute. A meal. A body that mostly works. A message from someone who cares. We receive, we move on, and the thank-you often gets left somewhere behind us on the road.

Luke tells a story about that very habit.

“And as he entered into a certain village, there met him ten men that were lepers, which stood afar off: and they lifted up their voices, and said, Jesus, Master, have mercy on us.” (Luke 17:12–13, KJV)

Ten men. Kept at a distance by their disease. Their only move left is to shout. They don’t ask for a lecture or a plan. They ask for mercy.

Jesus does not make them wait beside Him for a visible miracle. He sends them walking:

“And when he saw them, he said unto them, Go shew yourselves unto the priests. And it came to pass, that, as they went, they were cleansed.” (Luke 17:14, KJV)

The healing happens as they went. Faith here looks like obedience before the proof arrives. They start the journey still unclean, and somewhere on the road the shame falls away. Skin clears. Community opens again. Nine of them, we assume, keep going toward the priests, toward the paperwork, toward the restored life they had begged for.

One does something different.

The one who turned back

“And one of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, and with a loud voice glorified God, and fell down on his face at his feet, giving him thanks: and he was a Samaritan.” (Luke 17:15–16, KJV)

He saw that he was healed. Then he turned back. Then he glorified God with a loud voice — the same kind of volume he had used for “have mercy,” now spent on praise. Then he fell at Jesus’s feet and gave thanks.

Luke notes that he was a Samaritan, an outsider among outsiders. The one least expected to get the theology right is the one who gets the relationship right. Healing without thanksgiving is incomplete. The body can be cleaned while the heart stays on the road, already onto the next thing.

Jesus notices the empty space where gratitude should be:

“And Jesus answering said, Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine? There are not found that returned to give glory to God, save this stranger. And he said unto him, Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole.” (Luke 17:17–19, KJV)

Where are the nine? It is not a cold scolding. It is the ache of love that has given freely and still longs for fellowship. Nine received a gift. One returned to the Giver. And Jesus tells that one something the others may have missed: thy faith hath made thee whole. The nine got their skin back. The one who came back got Jesus.

A pause in the middle of the day

Midday is a natural place to ask the same question of ourselves. What has already been given today that we have used without naming? Strength for the morning. A kindness we swallowed without reply. Answered prayer we treated as coincidence.

Gratitude does not require a perfect day. The Samaritan was still an outsider in other people’s eyes when he turned back. He simply refused to let the gift erase the Giver from view.

You do not need a long speech. A quiet sentence is enough: Thank You, Jesus. That was You. Then, if you can, turn back in some small way — a text of thanks to a person God used, a moment on your knees before the next meeting, a deliberate naming of one mercy before you scroll past it.

The nine are not villains. They did what Jesus told them to do. But the story lingers on the one who paused. Holiness often looks like that pause: receiving, noticing, returning, and saying thank You to the One whose mercy made the road possible.

A midday prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for mercies I have already walked past today. Forgive me when I am among the nine — cleansed, busy, and forgetful. Teach me to turn back. Open my eyes to see what You have done, and give me a loud enough heart to glorify You and a humble enough heart to fall at Your feet. Amen.

A prayer

Jesus, I bring You the weight of this hour. Meet me in Luke 17:17, and let Your word rest in my heart. Give rest to my soul and strength for what remains.
