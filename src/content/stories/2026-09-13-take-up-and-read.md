Late in the fourth century, in a house garden in Milan, a restless teacher of rhetoric came to the end of his excuses.

His name was Augustine. He was not a stranger to truth. He had already left behind the Manichaean errors of his youth. He had sat under Ambrose’s preaching. He knew the Scriptures were not foolish. He believed that Christ was the Way. And still he delayed.

He describes that delay with a humility that still sounds familiar. He was like a sleeper who knows it is better to wake, yet loves the heavy comfort of the pillow. God called; Augustine answered with drowsy words: *soon*, *presently*, *leave me a little*. “But ‘presently, presently,’ had no present,” he later confessed, “and my ‘little while’ went on for a long while.”

What held him was not mainly intellectual doubt. It was a divided will. He wanted holiness — and he wanted the old habits that holiness would end. He knew the good; he could not yet wholly will it.

Then a visit broke open the day.

Ponticianus, an African countryman who served in the imperial court and was himself a baptized Christian, came to see Augustine and his friend Alypius. On the table lay a book. Pontitianus opened it, expecting perhaps some work of rhetoric, and found instead the Apostle Paul. Joy and surprise lit his face. From that discovery the conversation turned to Antony of Egypt — a name famous among Christ’s servants, yet unknown to Augustine until that hour — and to communities of prayer, poverty, and quiet obedience that were already springing up even near Milan.

Augustine listened, and something in him burned. Unlearned men and women were taking heaven by force, while he, with all his learning, still wallowed. The stories did not flatter him. They judged him kindly and severely at once.

When Ponticianus left, the storm broke.

Augustine turned on Alypius with words torn by shame and longing: Why do we delay? Why are we still here? The look of his face said more than his sentences. He fled into the little garden attached to their lodging; Alypius followed and sat nearby, astonished, giving his friend the gift of silent company.

There Augustine wrestled with himself. He wanted to enter God’s covenant with his whole heart — and found that wanting was not yet willing. His body obeyed the smallest impulse: tear the hair, clasp the knee. But the soul would not obey its own command to surrender. Two wills pulled in opposite directions, not because he was two persons, but because one will was still incomplete.

At last the misery rose from the deep places of his heart. Tears came in a flood. He rose from Alypius’s side and went farther into solitude, under a fig tree, and poured out what grief he could:

How long? How long — “tomorrow, and tomorrow”? Why not now? Why not this hour an end to uncleanness?

Then, from a neighboring house, he heard a voice — a child’s voice, boy or girl he could not tell — chanting again and again:

“Take up and read; Take up and read.”

(Augustine, Confessions VIII)

He stopped weeping and listened. Had children ever sung such words in a game? He could not remember hearing them. So he took the chant as a command from God: open the book; read the first passage you find.

He remembered Antony, who had once heard the Gospel read as if it were spoken to him alone — *Go, sell all… and come, follow me* — and had obeyed. Augustine hurried back to where Alypius still sat, where he had left the volume of Paul. He seized it, opened it, and read in silence the first words his eyes met:

“Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying; but put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, in concupiscence.”

(Augustine, Confessions VIII; citing Romans 13:13–14)

He read no further. He did not need to. “Instantly at the end of this sentence, by a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart, all the darkness of doubt vanished away.” (Augustine, Confessions VIII)

The long war of delay was over — not by Augustine’s cleverness, but by mercy meeting him in an ordinary garden, through a child’s chant and an open page.

He marked the place, closed the book, and told Alypius with a calm face what had happened. Alypius asked to see the text, read a little farther, found words that spoke to his own weaker faith, and joined him without turbulent delay. Together they went in to Monica, Augustine’s mother. She had prayed through years of tears. Now she rejoiced — more, Augustine says, than she had dared to ask — because God had given her son not only belief, but a will turned toward Him.

This is not a polished legend invented to impress. It is a man telling the truth about how hard it was to come, and how gently he was finally brought. Jesus met Augustine not as a theory but as a living Lord who interrupts delay: *Take up and read. Put on Christ. Why not now?*

If you are still saying *soon*, you are not alone in that garden. The same Christ still speaks through Scripture. You do not need a fig tree in Milan. You need an honest heart and an open page.

Take up and read. Ask Him for light. And when His word meets you, do not put off until tomorrow the surrender He offers today.
