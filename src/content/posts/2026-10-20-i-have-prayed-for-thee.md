Some evenings you replay the day and wince. The short answer you gave someone you love. The thing you promised yourself you wouldn't do again, and did. The moment you could have been brave and stayed quiet instead. By Tuesday night, the week already has a few of those in it.

Peter knew that feeling better than almost anyone in the Gospels. And on the night before the cross, Jesus told him something that is worth carrying to bed.

They were at the table. The bread had been broken, the cup passed. The disciples had just been arguing about which of them was the greatest. Into that room, Jesus turned to Peter:

> "And the Lord said, Simon, Simon, behold, Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat: But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not: and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren." (Luke 22:31–32, KJV)

Read it slowly. Jesus doesn't pretend the danger isn't real. A hard night is coming, a shaking that will toss Peter around like grain in a sieve. But before Peter has done anything wrong, before the courtyard and the fire and the servant girl's question, Jesus has already done something about it.

**"I have prayed for thee."**

Not "I will pray, if you fail." Not "I'll think about praying once you've shown you're sorry." It's already done. The prayer came first.

Peter, of course, didn't think he needed it:

> "And he said unto him, Lord, I am ready to go with thee, both into prison, and to death." (Luke 22:33, KJV)

He meant every word. That's the hard part. Most of our failures don't come from not caring. They come from loving Jesus with a love that turns out to be weaker than we thought. Peter wanted to be loyal. A few hours later, warming his hands by a fire, he said three times that he didn't know the man.

And then Luke gives us one of the most piercing lines in Scripture:

> "And the Lord turned, and looked upon Peter. And Peter remembered the word of the Lord... And Peter went out, and wept bitterly." (Luke 22:61–62, KJV)

What did Peter remember? Surely the warning about the rooster. But maybe also the rest of it: *I have prayed for thee. When thou art converted.* Not *if*. *When*. Jesus had seen the failure coming and had already planned for Peter's return. He had even given him a job for afterward: *strengthen thy brethren.* The fall wasn't going to be the end of Peter's story. It was going to become part of how he helped others.

That's exactly what happened. The man who denied Jesus by a fire became the man who stood up at Pentecost and preached. He had been sifted, and the faith Jesus prayed for had not failed.

Here's what this means for tonight. Jesus is still praying. Scripture says so plainly:

> "Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them." (Hebrews 7:25, KJV)

*Ever liveth.* Right now, while you lie awake thinking about what you said or didn't say, the risen Christ is not standing over you with a list. He is speaking to the Father on your behalf. Paul asked, "Who is he that condemneth?" and answered his own question: "It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us" (Romans 8:34, KJV).

So before you sleep, try this. Name one place today where you fell short. Just one, honestly, without dressing it up. Then picture Jesus turning toward you the way He turned toward Peter. Not to shame you, but to remind you: *I already prayed for this. I knew. I'm not letting go of you.*

Tell Him you're sorry. Then let Him have the last word, which is not your failure but His prayer.

And tomorrow, when you meet someone else who is falling apart over their own mistakes, you may find you have something to give them. That's what Jesus meant. The ones who have been carried are the ones who know how to strengthen their brothers and sisters.

Rest now. Your faith is held by more than your own grip on it.
