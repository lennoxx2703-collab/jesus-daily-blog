Some mornings you wake up already sitting by the side of the road. Life is moving past you. Other people seem to be going somewhere, and you're just trying to get through the day with what you have. You might not even know how to put into words what you need.

Mark tells us about a man in that exact spot. His name was Bartimaeus, a blind beggar sitting outside Jericho. Jesus was passing through with a large crowd on His way to Jerusalem, and Bartimaeus heard the noise.

> "When he heard that it was Jesus the Nazarene, he began to cry out, and say, 'Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!' Many rebuked him, that he should be quiet, but he cried out much more, 'You son of David, have mercy on me!'" (Mark 10:47–48)

## When the crowd says be quiet

The people around Bartimaeus told him to stop. Maybe they thought he was embarrassing. Maybe they thought Jesus had more important things to do. Whatever the reason, the message was clear: *people like you don't get to interrupt.*

You may have heard that voice too. Sometimes it comes from others, and sometimes it's your own. *Don't bother Him with this. It's too small. It's too late. You've asked before.* Bartimaeus heard all of that and cried out louder. His prayer was simple, and it's still one of the best prayers there is: *have mercy on me.*

You don't need polished words this morning. You only need to call out to the One who's passing by.

## Jesus stood still

Here is the line I can't get past: "Jesus stood still, and said, 'Call him'" (Mark 10:49).

Jesus was on His way to the cross. He had every reason to keep walking. And yet the whole procession stopped for one blind man shouting from the dust. The same crowd that had told him to be quiet now says, "Cheer up! Get up. He is calling you!"

That's the gospel in one small scene. You are not an interruption to Jesus. The cry you think is too much is the very thing that makes Him stop.

Bartimaeus didn't waste a second. "He, casting away his cloak, sprang up, and came to Jesus" (Mark 10:50). For a beggar, that cloak was probably his blanket, his coat, and the place where people dropped their coins. He left it behind the moment he was called.

## The question

Then Jesus asked him something that sounds almost too obvious:

> "What do you want me to do for you?" (Mark 10:51)

Surely Jesus could see what the man needed. But He asked anyway. He didn't treat Bartimaeus as a problem to fix. He treated him as a person to talk with. He invited him to say it out loud, to name the longing, to trust Him with it.

And Bartimaeus answered plainly: "Rabboni, that I may see again."

Jesus asks you the same question this morning. Not because He doesn't know, but because He wants you to come to Him honestly. What do you actually want Him to do? Not the tidy answer, the real one. Healing? Courage for a hard conversation? Peace about money? A way back to someone you love? To see Him again, when faith has gone dim?

Say it. He can handle the truth of your heart.

## Following on the way

Mark ends the story like this: "Jesus said to him, 'Go your way. Your faith has made you well.' Immediately he received his sight, and followed Jesus on the way" (Mark 10:52).

Jesus told him to go his way, and Bartimaeus chose Jesus' way. The first thing he did with new eyes was follow the One who had given them. That's where mercy leads: not only to relief, but to a life spent walking behind Jesus.

Here's a prayer to begin your Tuesday:

*Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me. I'm calling out to You, even if others think I should be quiet. You know what I need, but here it is in my own words. Help me throw off whatever I've been clinging to, and let me follow You on the way today.*

You may still be sitting by the road when you finish praying. That's all right. The road is exactly where Jesus walks, and He stops for those who call.
