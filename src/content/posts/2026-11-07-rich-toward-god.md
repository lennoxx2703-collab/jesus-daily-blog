Saturday midday has its own kind of noise. Errands, carts, screens full of things on sale, a quiet list in the back of the mind of what we still need, or think we need. None of it is wicked. Most of it is ordinary. But somewhere between the checkout and the scroll, a question can slip in without asking permission: *if I just had a little more, then I could rest.*

Jesus told a story about a man who believed exactly that.

> "And he said unto them, Take heed, and beware of covetousness: for a man's life consisteth not in the abundance of the things which he possesseth. And he spake a parable unto them, saying, The ground of a certain rich man brought forth plentifully: And he thought within himself, saying, What shall I do, because I have no room where to bestow my fruits? And he said, This will I do: I will pull down my barns, and build greater; and there will I bestow all my fruits and my goods. And I will say to my soul, Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry. But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided? So is he that layeth up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God." (Luke 12:15–21, KJV)

### He talked only to himself

Read the rich man's words slowly and notice who is in the conversation. *He thought within himself.* *I will pull down.* *I will say to my soul.* There is no neighbor in his plan, no thanks for the rain that grew the crop, no prayer. A good harvest was a gift, and he turned it into a closed room.

That is the quiet danger Jesus is naming. Not the barn. Not the harvest. The loneliness of a heart that only plans with itself.

### Life is not the inventory

"A man's life consisteth not in the abundance of the things which he possesseth." Jesus says it plainly, almost gently, before He tells the story. Your life is not the sum of what is in your cupboards or your account. It is not measured by your phone, your car, or how full the shelves look. Those things can serve a life. They cannot *be* one.

It is freeing to hear that on a Saturday. You do not have to buy your way into peace. You do not have to build a bigger barn before you are allowed to rest. The rest the rich man promised his soul — *take thine ease* — was something he could have had at Jesus' feet without a single new barn.

### What rich toward God looks like

Jesus doesn't end with a scolding; He ends with a direction. *Rich toward God.* That is a different kind of wealth, and anyone can start gathering it today.

It looks like gratitude spoken out loud for the ordinary harvest of this week. It looks like an open hand: groceries for a neighbor, a coin given without fuss, time handed to someone who is lonely. It looks like a heart that brings its plans to the Father before it brings them to the store. "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" (Matthew 6:21, KJV).

None of this means despising good things. It means holding them the way a child holds a gift, aware of the Giver, glad to share.

### For this Saturday

Before the afternoon fills up, try a small turn of the heart:

- Name three things you already have and thank God for them, specifically.
- Ask, *Lord, is there someone I could be generous to today?* and do the first thing that comes to mind.
- Let one purchase wait, and notice that you are still okay.

The rich man's question was *what shall I do with all of this?* Jesus offers a better one: *who is it all for?* When the answer is Him, and the people He loves, even a small barn becomes a place of overflowing.

*Lord Jesus, You are my treasure. Loosen my grip on what I have, open my hands to the people around me, and make me rich toward God today. Amen.*
