It's the middle of Monday. The list that looked manageable at breakfast has grown teeth. Messages are stacking up, someone needs an answer by three, and the thought of stopping for anything — lunch, a breath, a prayer — feels like a luxury you can't afford.

Luke tells us about a moment in Jesus' life that looked a lot like that.

> "But so much the more went there a fame abroad of him: and great multitudes came together to hear, and to be healed by him of their infirmities. And he withdrew himself into the wilderness, and prayed."
> — Luke 5:15–16 (KJV)

## The demand was real

Notice what was happening. This wasn't a slow week. Jesus had just touched a leper and made him clean, and the news spread faster than anyone could control. *So much the more* — the crowds were bigger than ever. And they weren't coming for trivial reasons. They were sick. They were hurting. They wanted to hear Him.

If anyone ever had a good excuse to skip prayer because there was simply too much important work to do, it was Jesus.

And that's exactly when He withdrew.

## Stepping away isn't stepping out

The word Luke uses suggests a habit, not a one-off: He *kept* withdrawing. It was a rhythm. When the pull of people was strongest, Jesus went somewhere quiet and talked with His Father.

That tells us something freeing. Withdrawing to pray wasn't Jesus abandoning the crowds. It was how He stayed able to love them. The same Jesus who slipped away to the wilderness came back and healed a paralyzed man let down through a roof (Luke 5:18–25). The quiet wasn't a detour from His mission. It was part of it.

Mark records Him doing the same thing with His tired disciples: *"Come ye yourselves apart into a desert place, and rest a while: for there were many coming and going, and they had no leisure so much as to eat"* (Mark 6:31). Jesus noticed they hadn't even eaten. He didn't push them harder. He invited them away.

## Your wilderness can be small

You probably can't walk into an actual wilderness at 1:30 on a Monday. That's okay. Jesus' wilderness was simply a place where the noise couldn't follow Him. Yours might be:

- a short walk around the block with your phone in your pocket,
- your car in the parking lot for five minutes before going back in,
- a closed door, a glass of water, and one honest sentence to God.

It doesn't need to be long or polished. *"Father, there's a lot today. I'm here. Help me."* That's a prayer. Jesus taught us to call Him Father precisely so we wouldn't have to perform.

## What you're really saying

When you step away to pray in the middle of a busy day, you're confessing something true: *I am not the one holding all of this together.* That can feel risky. What if things fall apart while you're gone?

But the crowds didn't vanish while Jesus prayed. The needs were still there when He came back — and so was the power to meet them. *"Be still, and know that I am God"* (Psalm 46:10). Stillness is not neglect. It's remembering who's actually in charge.

## For the rest of today

So here's a gentle invitation for this Monday midday: before you answer the next message, withdraw for a moment. Not because you've finished everything, but because you haven't. Let Jesus, who knew what it was to be pressed on every side, show you the way He walked through it — by going to His Father first.

The list will still be there. But you won't be carrying it alone.
