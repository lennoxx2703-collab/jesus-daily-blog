Midweek mornings have a way of making us count. Who got noticed, who got the credit, who's ahead, who's behind. Maybe it's a promotion someone else got, or a group chat where you feel like an afterthought. Before the day starts its scoring, let's sit with two of Jesus' closest friends who wanted, very badly, to win.

> "And James and John, the sons of Zebedee, come unto him, saying, Master, we would that thou shouldest do for us whatsoever we shall desire. And he said unto them, What would ye that I should do for you? They said unto him, Grant unto us that we may sit, one on thy right hand, and the other on thy left hand, in thy glory." — Mark 10:35–37 (KJV)

It's almost funny how honest they are. *Do for us whatever we ask.* Not a small request, either: the two best seats in the kingdom. And they ask it right after Jesus has told them, for the third time, that He is going to Jerusalem to suffer and die (Mark 10:33–34). He's talking about a cross. They're talking about chairs.

Before we roll our eyes, though, we should admit we know this conversation from the inside. Most of us have prayed some version of it: *Lord, bless my plans, and make sure I end up somewhere that matters.* And the other ten disciples weren't better — Mark says that when they heard, "they began to be much displeased with James and John" (Mark 10:41). Not because the request was wrong, probably, but because they hadn't thought of it first.

## He doesn't shame them

What strikes me is how gently Jesus answers. No lecture, no sarcasm. "Ye know not what ye ask" (Mark 10:38). He calls the whole group together and quietly redraws the map:

> "But so shall it not be among you: but whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister: and whosoever of you will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all. For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many." — Mark 10:43–45 (KJV)

Notice He doesn't tell them to stop wanting greatness. He tells them where it actually is. The way up in His kingdom runs downward — toward the person who needs a hand, the task nobody wants, the kindness that won't be seen. "Minister" here simply means servant, the one who waits on others.

And then He points to Himself. Not as a rule-giver standing above the rule, but as the clearest example of it. The One who truly deserved every seat of honor came to serve, and to give His very life *for* us. The ransom wasn't paid by someone climbing. It was paid by Someone kneeling, and then by Someone on a cross.

## The seats on His right and left

There's a quiet ache in this story. A few chapters later, at the crucifixion, Mark tells us there were two men on Jesus' right and left — two thieves (Mark 15:27). That was what "glory" looked like that day. James and John didn't know what they were asking. Jesus did. And He went anyway, so that people who argue over seats could be welcomed to His table at all.

That changes how I want to walk into today. My worth isn't decided by where I sit in the meeting, how many people respond to my message, or whether anyone notices what I did. It was settled by a Servant who gave Himself for me. Since that's settled, I'm free — free to hold a door, to make the coffee, to listen longer than I planned, to let someone else have the credit.

## For today

Maybe pray something simple this morning:

*Lord Jesus, You came not to be served but to serve. I'm tired of counting. Show me one person today I can quietly serve, and let that be enough. Thank You that You gave Your life for me before I ever did a thing for You. Amen.*

Then go look for that one person. You won't get a better seat for it. But you might find yourself sitting a little closer to Him.
