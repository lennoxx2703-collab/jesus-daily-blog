Saturday at noon is often when we're finally around the people we love most. The house is full, or the table is, or the group chat is busy. And somewhere between lunch and the afternoon, it's easy to start noticing things. The way someone left the kitchen. The tone of a reply. The habit in a friend or a spouse or a sibling that we've pointed out more than once, at least in our heads.

Jesus has a word for that moment, and it's one of His most vivid:

> "And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother's eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye? Or how wilt thou say to thy brother, Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye; and, behold, a beam is in thine own eye? Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye." — Matthew 7:3–5 (KJV)

## A carpenter's picture

Remember who is talking. The people of Nazareth once asked, "Is not this the carpenter?" (Mark 6:3). Jesus had spent years around sawdust and timber. He knew what a splinter felt like, and He knew what a roof beam weighed.

So He paints a picture that is almost funny: a man with a whole plank sticking out of his face, leaning in carefully toward his brother, squinting, saying, *Hold still — I just need to get that tiny speck out.* Everyone listening would have laughed. And then, probably, gone quiet. Because we've all been that man.

The trouble isn't that we see too much. It's that we see in only one direction. Other people's specks look enormous; our own beams feel like part of the furniture.

## Not "never help," but "help from a clear eye"

It would be easy to hear this passage as *mind your own business, never say anything.* But look at the last line again: "and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye."

Jesus isn't against helping your brother. He actually expects it. He just knows that help offered from a blind spot usually wounds instead of heals. Paul says the same thing later: "if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted" (Galatians 6:1). *Considering thyself* — that's the beam work.

And the order matters. First me, then them. First the honest look inward, then the gentle word outward.

## Letting Him work on your eye

Here's the good news hidden in this hard saying: you don't have to pull the beam out alone. The One who described it is the same One who heals eyes. He spat on the ground and opened blind eyes; He can certainly reach the plank in yours.

Taking the beam out usually doesn't look dramatic. It sounds more like a quiet prayer at the sink: *Lord, before I say anything to them — what in me needs You right now?* Maybe it's tiredness that's making everything irritating. Maybe it's the very same fault, just dressed differently. Maybe it's a hurt from earlier in the week that has nothing to do with them at all.

When you let Jesus answer that question, something softens. The speck in the other person is still there, maybe. But now you see it with the same mercy you've just received. You remember that "with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again" (Matthew 7:2) — and you'd rather be measured with grace.

## For this afternoon

If someone has been getting under your skin today, try this before you speak:

- **Pause and name your own beam.** Ask Jesus to show you one thing in you, not in them.
- **Receive His mercy first.** He doesn't pull planks out to shame us; He does it so we can see.
- **Then, if it still needs saying, say it gently.** As someone who has also been helped.

The carpenter from Nazareth knows wood, and He knows hearts. Let Him start with yours this afternoon. You'll be surprised how much clearer everyone else looks.
