It's the middle of a Monday. The coffee has worn off, the inbox has refilled itself, and maybe the thing you worked hardest on this morning didn't land. If that's you, there's a fisherman in Luke 5 who would understand.

Simon Peter had been out on the lake of Gennesaret all night. That was the job: nets in the dark, cold hands, hauling and resetting until dawn. And that night it had all come to nothing. By morning he was on the shore, washing empty nets. Tired work, after wasted work.

That's when Jesus stepped into his boat.

> "And he entered into one of the ships, which was Simon's, and prayed him that he would thrust out a little from the land. And he sat down, and taught the people out of the ship." (Luke 5:3, KJV)

Notice where Jesus chose to meet him. Not in the synagogue, not on a mountain, but in Peter's workplace, in Peter's boat, in the middle of Peter's bad shift. Jesus borrowed the very thing that had failed Peter all night and turned it into a pulpit.

Then, when the teaching was over, He turned to Peter:

> "Launch out into the deep, and let down your nets for a draught. And Simon answering said unto him, Master, we have toiled all the night, and have taken nothing: nevertheless at thy word I will let down the net." (Luke 5:4–5, KJV)

I love how honest Peter is. He doesn't pretend. He's the professional here; Jesus is a carpenter's son. Fish don't come up in broad daylight, and Peter has the empty nets to prove it. *We have toiled all the night, and have taken nothing.* That's a real report from a real tired man.

But then comes the hinge word: **nevertheless.**

Peter doesn't have to feel hopeful to obey. He doesn't have to understand the plan. He just takes his tiredness and his experience and his doubt, sets them down next to Jesus' word, and lets the word win. *Nevertheless at thy word.*

> "And when they had this done, they inclosed a great multitude of fishes: and their net brake." (Luke 5:6, KJV)

More than the nets could hold. More than the boat could carry; they had to call their partners over. And Peter's response isn't a celebration. He falls down at Jesus' knees: *"Depart from me; for I am a sinful man, O Lord"* (Luke 5:8). He suddenly realizes who has been sitting in his boat all morning.

Jesus doesn't leave. He says, *"Fear not"* (Luke 5:10), and gives Peter a bigger calling than he came with.

### For the rest of your day

A few things to carry into this afternoon:

**Jesus isn't only in your quiet time. He's in your work.** The desk, the shift, the kitchen, the classroom, the car. He is perfectly comfortable climbing into the boat you're working from, even if it has been a bad night in that boat.

**You're allowed to be honest about the empty nets.** Peter said it plainly. You can too: *Lord, I worked hard and it came to nothing.* He can take that sentence.

**Obedience can be small and tired.** Peter's "nevertheless" wasn't a big spiritual feeling. It was a weary man lowering a net one more time because Jesus asked. Maybe your "nevertheless" this afternoon is one more honest email, one more patient answer to your kid, one more prayer when you'd rather not.

**The catch isn't the point; He is.** The fish were wonderful, but what changed Peter's life was seeing Jesus clearly. Whatever this afternoon brings, full nets or empty ones, the One in your boat is the real gift.

So before you go back to it, take a breath and pray something like this:

*Lord Jesus, I'm tired, and some of my effort today feels wasted. Nevertheless, at Your word, I'll let down the net again. Get in my boat. Be with me in my work this afternoon, and let me see You more clearly than I see the results. Amen.*
