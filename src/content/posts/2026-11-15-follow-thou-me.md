Midday is when the scrolling usually starts. A few minutes of rest, a phone in hand, and suddenly you're looking at someone else's life — their family, their job, their peace, their prayers answered. And something small and quiet tightens in your chest: *Why them? Why not me? What about my road?*

If that's where you are right now, you're in good company. Peter was there too, on a beach, with Jesus standing right in front of him.

## A conversation on the shore

It was after the resurrection. Jesus had cooked breakfast over a fire of coals. He had asked Peter three times, "Lovest thou me?" — one for every denial — and three times He had given Peter his work back: *Feed my sheep.* Then Jesus told Peter, honestly and tenderly, that his road would not be easy. It would end in a death that would glorify God. And then the two words that started it all: *Follow me.*

And right then, Peter turned around.

> "Then Peter, turning about, seeth the disciple whom Jesus loved following... Peter seeing him saith to Jesus, Lord, and what shall this man do? Jesus saith unto him, If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee? follow thou me." — John 21:20–22 (KJV)

Think about how human that is. Peter has just been restored, forgiven, commissioned — and his very next move is to look over his shoulder at John. *Okay, Lord, but what about him? Is his road harder or easier? Does he get the same?*

## Jesus doesn't answer the comparison

Notice what Jesus does. He doesn't explain John's future. He doesn't reassure Peter that John's road will be just as hard, or promise that it will all feel fair in the end. He simply refuses to let John's story become Peter's question.

"What is that to thee? follow thou me."

That can sound sharp at first. But listen again, and it's one of the kindest things Jesus could say. He is saying: *Peter, I didn't call you to manage John's story. I called you to walk with Me. Your eyes are on the wrong face.*

Comparison always pulls our eyes off Jesus and onto somebody else. It makes us either proud or bitter — proud when we seem ahead, bitter when we seem behind. Neither one leaves any room for joy. Paul put it plainly: "Let every man prove his own work, and then shall he have rejoicing in himself alone, and not in another" (Galatians 6:4, KJV).

## Your road has your name on it

Here's the freeing part. Jesus didn't give Peter and John the same road, because He didn't make them the same person. Peter would lead and suffer and die young. John would live long and write and remember. Both were loved. Both were called. Neither road was a mistake.

The same is true for you. Your timing, your struggles, the unanswered prayer you're carrying, the gift you have that someone else doesn't — none of it is Jesus forgetting you. It's your road. And He walks it with you personally, not from a distance like a scorekeeper.

The writer of Hebrews tells us to "run with patience the race that is set before us, looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith" (Hebrews 12:1–2, KJV). *Set before us* — not before someone else. And *looking unto Jesus* — not looking sideways.

## For the rest of today

Maybe put the phone down for a minute. And when you catch yourself turning around to check someone else's path this afternoon, try this small prayer:

*"Lord, what is that to me? You're here. I'll follow You."*

You don't have to understand anyone else's story to be faithful in yours. You only have to keep your eyes on the One who said your name on the shore and then said, again, *Follow me.*

He hasn't stopped saying it. And He hasn't stopped walking ahead of you.
