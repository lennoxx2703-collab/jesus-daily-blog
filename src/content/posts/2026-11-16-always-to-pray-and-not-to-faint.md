There's a particular kind of tiredness that has nothing to do with the workday. It's the tiredness of praying the same prayer again tonight. The same name. The same diagnosis. The same marriage, the same child, the same bill, the same ache you've brought to God so many evenings that you can almost hear yourself starting to sound like a broken record.

Somewhere in there a quiet thought shows up: *Maybe I should stop asking. Maybe I'm bothering Him.*

Jesus knew that thought would come. So He told a story with a very clear label on it.

> "And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint; saying, There was in a city a judge, which feared not God, neither regarded man: and there was a widow in that city; and she came unto him, saying, Avenge me of mine adversary. And he would not for a while: but afterward he said within himself, Though I fear not God, nor regard man; yet because this widow troubleth me, I will avenge her, lest by her continual coming she weary me."
> — Luke 18:1–5 (KJV)

## A story with the point printed on the front

Most parables make you work for the meaning. This one doesn't. Luke tells us the purpose before the story even starts: *that men ought always to pray, and not to faint.*

To faint here means to lose heart. To give up. To let your hands drop. Jesus isn't scolding people who are tired; He's talking straight to them. He saw the disciples, and He sees us, and He knows that the hardest part of prayer is often not the first time we ask. It's the fortieth.

## The widow had nothing but showing up

In that world a widow had almost no power. No husband to speak for her, often no money for a bribe, no influence in the court. The judge she faced didn't care about God and didn't care about people. Humanly speaking, her case was hopeless.

All she had was her persistence. She kept coming. Morning after morning she was there again, saying the same thing. And eventually even a hard, selfish man gave in, just to get some peace.

It's almost funny. Jesus is painting a picture of a judge who is worn down by a widow who simply will not go away.

## Now the turn

Then Jesus says the part that matters most:

> "And the Lord said, Hear what the unjust judge saith. And shall not God avenge his own elect, which cry day and night unto him, though he bear long with them?"
> — Luke 18:6–7 (KJV)

This is not a story where God is the judge. It's a story where God is the *opposite* of the judge. That's the whole argument. If a man who doesn't care at all will finally listen to a stranger who keeps knocking, how much more will your Father, who loves you, hear His own children who cry to Him day and night?

You are not a stranger at His door. You are not an annoyance He's trying to get rid of. You're the one He calls His own.

## When it feels long

Jesus doesn't pretend the waiting isn't real. He even uses the phrase *though he bear long with them.* Some prayers take a long time. The Psalms are full of people who said so out loud: "How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever?" (Psalm 13:1). That's in the Bible. God kept it there on purpose, so you'd know that honest, tired, repeated prayer is not a lack of faith. It *is* faith. You're still talking to Him.

And when you don't even have new words tonight, you're not on your own: "the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered" (Romans 8:26). The same old sentence, whispered one more time before you fall asleep, is carried by Someone who knows exactly what you mean.

## For tonight

So bring it again. The same name, the same need. You don't need to dress it up or find a cleverer way to say it. Just come back, like the widow did, but with far more confidence than she had, because you know who's listening.

Persistence in prayer isn't about wearing God down. It's about not letting your heart wear down. Every time you come back, you're saying: *I still believe You're good. I still believe You hear me.*

Jesus told this story so you wouldn't quit. Don't quit tonight.

Lord Jesus, You know how many times I've brought this to You. I'm tired, but I'm still here. Keep my heart from fainting. Thank You that You are not a hard judge but a loving Father, and that You hear me tonight. Amen.
