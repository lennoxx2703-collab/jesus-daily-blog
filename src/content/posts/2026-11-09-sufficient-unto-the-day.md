Monday has a way of arriving all at once. Before your feet touch the floor, the whole week is already standing in the room: the meeting you dread, the bill that is due, the conversation you have been putting off, the list that did not get shorter over the weekend. You have not done a single thing yet, and somehow you are already tired.

Jesus knew that feeling. He spoke to people who worked with their hands, who did not know whether the harvest would come in, who carried tomorrow on their shoulders every single day. And on a hillside in Galilee He said this:

> *But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you. Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof.*
> — Matthew 6:33–34 (KJV)

## First things first

Notice that Jesus does not begin with "stop worrying." He begins with "seek." He does not just take something away from us; He gives us somewhere to put our attention.

To seek the kingdom first is not to add one more task to the morning, as if prayer were item zero on the to-do list. It is to remember who is King before the day starts telling you who is in charge. The inbox is not king. The boss is not king. The fear is not king. Your Father is, and He already knows what you need — Jesus said so just a breath earlier: *your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things* (Matthew 6:32).

So a Monday can start with something very simple: *Lord, this day is Yours before it is mine. Show me what matters in it.*

## Only today

Then comes one of the kindest sentences Jesus ever spoke: *Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof.* Each day has enough trouble of its own.

That is not pessimism. It is mercy. Jesus is being honest — yes, today will have its weight — and He is also drawing a line around it. You are not asked to carry Wednesday on Monday. You are not given strength for Friday's problem this morning, because Friday's problem is not here yet, and neither is Friday's grace.

Most of our anxiety lives in a day that does not exist. We rehearse arguments that may never happen. We pay, in advance and with interest, for troubles that may never arrive. And the cost comes straight out of today — out of the people in front of us, the work in our hands, the quiet moments where God is actually near.

The prophet Jeremiah, writing from the ruins of a city, still found this to be true: *his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness* (Lamentations 3:22–23). God's mercy comes the way manna came in the wilderness — enough for the day, fresh each morning, never stored up in a jar for later. You will not have tomorrow's mercy today. But you will have it tomorrow.

## A Monday practice

Maybe try this before the week gets loud:

- **Name what is actually today's.** Write down only what belongs to this day. Let the rest wait on the next page.
- **Hand the rest over.** For each thing that belongs to later, say plainly, *Father, You hold this until it is time.*
- **Seek one kingdom thing.** One kindness, one honest word, one moment of attention to someone who needs it. Small is fine. The kingdom grows from mustard seeds.

None of this makes the week light. But it makes it walkable — one day at a time, with Jesus beside you in each one.

## A prayer for the start of the week

*Lord Jesus, You see everything waiting for me this week, and none of it surprises You. Teach me to seek Your kingdom first today, not as a duty but as a relief. Take tomorrow out of my hands and give me grace for this one day. Let me be fully present to the people You put in front of me. And when worry comes back — and it will — turn my eyes again to You. Amen.*

Today is enough. And He is enough for today.
