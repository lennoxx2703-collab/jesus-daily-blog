Most of us have learned how to stand in a room so that people don't notice the part of us that isn't working.

Maybe it's a habit you can't seem to break, a marriage that's quieter than it looks, a faith that's gone thin, a body that won't do what it used to. You keep it at your side. You angle yourself a little. You get really good at being fine. And Friday morning is a natural time to keep doing that — one more day, one more meeting, and then the weekend.

There's a man in Mark's Gospel who knew how to stand like that.

> "And he entered again into the synagogue; and there was a man there which had a withered hand." — Mark 3:1 (KJV)

## A man in the back of the room

Mark doesn't give us his name. Just the hand. It was "withered" — dried up, shrunken, useless. In a world where most work was done with your hands, that meant a smaller life. Less work, less income, maybe less respect. I imagine he'd learned to keep it tucked into his robe.

And on this particular Sabbath, the room wasn't safe. Mark says the religious leaders "watched him, whether he would heal him on the sabbath day; that they might accuse him" (Mark 3:2). The man with the withered hand had become a test case. Nobody was looking at him as a person. They were looking at him as a trap.

Except Jesus.

## "Stand forth"

> "And he saith unto the man which had the withered hand, Stand forth." — Mark 3:3 (KJV)

Literally, *rise up into the middle.* Jesus doesn't heal him quietly in a corner. He calls him out of the edges and into the center of the room, in front of everybody.

That can sound frightening if you've spent years hiding something. But notice what Jesus is doing. He's taking the man out of the role of test case and putting him where he belongs — at the center of God's attention. The leaders wanted to argue about rules. Jesus wanted this man whole. He even asks them straight out: "Is it lawful to do good on the sabbath days, or to do evil? to save life, or to kill?" And "they held their peace" (Mark 3:4).

Then Mark tells us something remarkable about Jesus' heart. He "looked round about on them with anger, being grieved for the hardness of their hearts" (Mark 3:5). Jesus gets angry — not at the man who's broken, but at the people who'd rather keep him broken than see their system disturbed. If you've ever been made to feel like your struggle was an inconvenience to religious people, hear this: it grieved Jesus too.

## "Stretch forth thine hand"

> "He saith unto the man, Stretch forth thine hand. And he stretched it out: and his hand was restored whole as the other." — Mark 3:5 (KJV)

Here's the part I can't get past. Jesus asks the man to do the one thing he couldn't do. Stretch out a withered hand. Hold out the very part of you that doesn't work, in front of the whole room.

And somewhere between the command and the stretching, the healing came. The man didn't fix his hand and then present it. He obeyed with it still broken, and it was restored as he reached.

That's often how it goes with Jesus. He doesn't ask us to bring Him the healed version. He asks us to stop hiding the withered thing and hold it out to Him. Sometimes that means telling Him honestly in prayer. Sometimes it means telling one trusted person. Sometimes it means taking one small step of obedience before you feel ready.

## For this Friday

Before the day picks up speed, ask yourself: *What have I been keeping at my side?* You don't have to announce it to the world. Just stop angling away from Him.

*Lord Jesus, You see the part of me I've learned to hide. I'm tired of standing at the edges. I'm holding it out to You now — not fixed, not pretty, just honest. Do what only You can do. Make me whole. Amen.*

He's not watching you to accuse you. He's calling you into the middle because He wants you well.
