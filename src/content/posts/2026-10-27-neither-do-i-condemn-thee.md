Midday has a way of bringing out the accuser. The morning's good intentions have already slipped a little. You snapped at someone, or missed something, or remembered an old thing you'd rather forget. And somewhere in your head a voice lines up the evidence and asks what you have to say for yourself.

John tells a story about a woman who knew that voice from the outside.

> "And the scribes and Pharisees brought unto him a woman taken in adultery; and when they had set her in the midst, they say unto him, Master, this woman was taken in adultery, in the very act. Now Moses in the law commanded us, that such should be stoned: but what sayest thou?" (John 8:3–5, KJV)

She is set "in the midst." Everyone can see her. Nobody asks her name. She isn't a person to them, she's a test case, a way to trap Jesus. That's what shame does: it puts you in the middle of the room and turns your worst moment into your whole identity.

And then Jesus does something strange.

> "But Jesus stooped down, and with his finger wrote on the ground, as though he heard them not." (John 8:6, KJV)

We don't know what He wrote. People have guessed for centuries. But notice what the stooping does. Every eye in that courtyard had been on her. Now they're on Him, bent down in the dust. He takes the attention off her and onto Himself. Before He says a word, He has already stepped between her and the crowd.

When they keep pressing, He stands up and says one sentence:

> "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her." (John 8:7, KJV)

Then He stoops again and goes back to writing, and the courtyard goes quiet. "Beginning at the eldest, even unto the last," they drop their stones and walk away. The oldest ones go first. Maybe they simply had the longest memories of their own failures.

Then comes the part that gets me every time:

> "When Jesus had lifted up himself, and saw none but the woman, he said unto her, Woman, where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee? She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." (John 8:10–11, KJV)

The only one in that courtyard who actually *was* without sin, the only one with the right to throw a stone, is the one who doesn't. He lets the accusers have their say, then He gets the last word. And His last word is not "guilty." It's "neither do I."

Notice the order, too. He doesn't say "sin no more, and then I won't condemn you." Freedom comes first. "Go, and sin no more" isn't the price of His mercy. It's the life that mercy makes possible. You can't really walk away from something while you're still pinned in the middle of the room. He lifts you out first, and then He says go.

So if the accuser has been busy in your head this morning, try this over lunch. Picture the stones going down. Picture the voices walking off one by one, until it's just you and Jesus left standing in the quiet. Then let Him ask you, gently, "Where are those thine accusers?" And hear His answer for you:

> "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus." (Romans 8:1, KJV)

Not less condemnation. Not condemnation on probation. None.

You can take the afternoon from there. Not as a test case in the middle of the room, but as someone Jesus has already stood up for. Go on. He's said His word, and it's the last one.
